# Case study — Form / Flow

## At a glance

**Role explored:** AI Integration Specialist, Industrial Design  
**Domain:** Electric mobility · industrial design · UI/UX · design operations  
**Artifact:** Responsive, interactive browser prototype  
**Status:** Student portfolio concept; no access to company staff, internal data or production AI services.

## The opportunity

Industrial designers move between open-ended exploration and precise delivery. Useful context can be split across a brief, sketches, material studies, interface discussions and engineering reviews. Repeated coordination work can pull attention away from design decisions.

This concept explores one plausible opportunity from the job description: make the path from brief to team review easier to follow, and let AI assist with framing and documentation while leaving authorship and judgment with the design team.

## The project prompt

Reimagine an urban electric commute for first-time EV riders. Build confidence on unfamiliar routes, make the vehicle intuitive at a glance, and carry everyday essentials without visual clutter.

### Principles

1. **Confidence first:** make important information easy to understand.
2. **Quiet utility:** make useful details feel integrated rather than added on.
3. **Warm precision:** pair considered geometry with approachable material cues.

These are design prompts for this project, not claims about validated rider preferences.

## The experience

The Studio page puts the challenge beside three original vehicle form directions, an early dashboard hierarchy sketch and a small AI assistant panel. Four stages give the team a shared sense of where work sits. The Brief page keeps the challenge editable. Process Insights shows how synthetic measures could be presented without implying that they came from a real study. The Playbook page captures how to explore a tool, check it and document its limits.

### Vehicle design

The three vector scooter studies use different visual territories: soft and reassuring, expressive and modular, and quieter and more minimal. They are intentionally early form studies. They are not production-ready CAD, engineering validation, or claims about feasibility.

### UI/UX design

The small instrument panel preview prioritizes remaining range, then a next destination cue. This is a hierarchy hypothesis for discussion, not a usability-tested screen. A real next step would put it in context with riders and test glanceability, legibility and distraction.

### AI integration

Flow Assist accepts a brief-related prompt and returns a local example response. The interface names the prototype mode and the response reminds the user to review suggestions. A real implementation would need a selected service, data handling review, evaluation examples, failure handling and designer feedback before use.

## Workflow and interaction choices

- **Keep the brief in view:** prompts are anchored to the design challenge rather than an empty chat.
- **Offer small starting points:** quick actions help explore themes, risks and handoff tasks.
- **Make decisions visible:** concept cards can be selected and saved; stage navigation communicates progress.
- **Preserve designer agency:** suggestions are framed as hypotheses and review prompts.
- **Make the prototype inspectable:** edit the brief, export a JSON summary, and use clear local-demo labels.

## What is and is not demonstrated

Demonstrated: front-end interaction design, responsive UI, vector vehicle concept visuals, an interface hierarchy sketch, local browser persistence, a rule-based assistant prototype and clear documentation.

Not demonstrated: interviews, observed team bottlenecks, actual AI model integration, quantitative productivity gains, validated rider research, production vehicle geometry or engineering feasibility. The process insights are fictional sample data and should never be presented as measured outcomes.

## Next validation steps

1. Interview industrial, vehicle, CMF and UI/UX designers about a recent concept-to-review workflow.
2. Map repeated tasks, tools, handoffs and the cost of rework; separate observed facts from assumptions.
3. Choose one low-risk, frequent task and compare a manual workflow with two candidate AI-assisted approaches.
4. Evaluate usefulness, editability, time-to-review, output quality, privacy and failure recovery with the people doing the work.
5. Revise the prototype based on feedback; only then consider connecting a real service or integrating with an existing design tool.

## Reflection

The strongest contribution of an AI workflow tool may be better context and smoother handoffs, not more generated imagery. The concept therefore treats vehicle design, rider UI and process support as parts of one design conversation and keeps the final call with people.
